## 1. Chord → Scale Correspondence

### 1.1 Modes of the major scale

| Mode | Chord | Avoid notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ionian | △7 | **4** (clashes with the major third, a semitone apart) |
| Dorian | m7 | 6 (depends on context) |
| Phrygian | m7 / Sus♭9 | ♭2 (it is also a characteristic note) |
| **Lydian** | **△7(♯11)** | ★ **None**. This is why it sounds more "open" than Ionian |
| **Mixolydian** | **Dominant 7** | 4 |
| Aeolian | m7 | ♭6 |
| **Locrian** | **m7(♭5)** | ♭2 |

★ **"Avoid notes" does not mean they cannot be used; it means you should not stop on them.**

### 1.2 The seven modes of the melodic minor (★ the core resource of jazz)

| Degree | Name | Chord |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Melodic Minor | **mMaj7** (minor-major 7) |
| 2 | Dorian ♭2 | **Sus♭9** |
| 3 | Lydian Augmented | **△7♯5** |
| 4 | **Lydian Dominant** | **7(♯11)** ★ the easiest to carry into pop |
| 5 | Mixolydian ♭6 | 7(♭13) |
| 6 | Locrian ♮2 | **m7(♭5)** |
| 7 | **Altered (Super Locrian)** | ★ **7alt**, the core of the jazz sound |

★★ **Interchangeability**: the chords generated by the same melodic minor can substitute for one another.
**Think of "which melodic minor we are currently on", not of individual chords.**

### 1.3 Diminished and whole-tone scales

| Scale | Used over | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| **Half-whole diminished** | V7(♭9) | ★ The diminished seventh chord is a "disguise" for V7♭9; after bebop, V7♭9 is used directly |
| **Whole-half diminished** | dim7 | ★ **Repeats in minor thirds** → one voicing works for several V7♭9 chords |
| **Whole-tone scale** | 7(♯5), augmented chords | |

## 2. Levine's Details: The Points SKILL §3 Does Not Expand On (p0055–0110)

### 2.1 Correct use of avoid notes (p0055–0059)

Levine himself says "avoid note" is a poor term; a more accurate one is **"handle with care"** (p0055). The 4th over I△7, played quickly as a passing note, is barely heard as dissonant. When it is used deliberately, **it usually resolves down to the 3rd** (E♭ to D in bar 9 of "Stella By Starlight", p0056). In outside passages, or when you stay on one △7 for a long time, the 4th may be the most interesting note (p0056). The 4th over a dominant 7 (the 11th) works the same way: you can play it and then move down a semitone to the 3rd (p0059).
**Do not treat consonance as good and dissonance as bad. Dissonance supplies tension, resolution, and energy** (p0056, p0059).

### 2.2 When to use Lydian and when not to (p0057–0058)

- You do not have to wait for a ♯4 to appear in the chord symbol before raising it; almost any △7 can take it. **A △7 on IV sounds especially good.** Fake books often leave it unmarked, because it is optional (p0058).
- Lydian can be "out of character" over pop tunes; Oliver Nelson used it in his arrangement of *Yesterday* (p0057). It is not a modern invention: the first chord of the bridge of *Someone To Watch Over Me* (1926) and bar 6 of *Happy Birthday* (1893) both use it (p0058).
- Notation: it is a raised 4th, not a lowered 5th. Before the 1960s it was written as ♭5; afterward, with modal thinking, it came to be written as ♯4 / ♯11 (p0057, p0060).

### 2.3 How to voice sus chords (p0061, p0064–0065)

Definition: **"a V-degree chord in which a 4th sounds not like an avoid note"** (p0061).
- Voicing: put in the right hand **a major triad a whole step below the root** (for Dsus, a C major triad in the right hand), with one note doubled at the octave to thicken it (p0064).
- After the 1960s, **the 3rd and 4th may sound together, but the 3rd must sit above the 4th**. Reversing them produces a minor ninth, "the last dissonant interval" (p0064–0065).
- In *In Your Own Sweet Way*, Miles Davis alternates A♭sus with A♭7♯11 (p0064).

### 2.4 Melodic minor: the limits of interchangeability and characteristic notes (p0091–0095)

- Interchangeability holds because the melodic minor **has no avoid notes**. Every chord within the same key has the identical set of pitches, so any phrase or voicing over F-△ can be moved directly onto Gsus9, A♭△♯5, B♭7♯11, Dø, or E7alt (p0091).
- ★ **This does not apply to major keys.** D-7 and C△ both come from the key of C major, but the voicing for D-7 contains F, which is an avoid note over C△ (p0091).
- "Think key, not chord": you are playing the whole key, not the chord (p0092).
- **Characteristic notes**: the combinations 3, 5, 7, 9 and 1, 3, 5, 7 belong only to this one melodic minor. No other melodic minor and no major key has them. Playing them immediately establishes the tonality (p0095).
- Memory aid: a key signature on the piano means "play only the white keys except for certain notes". G melodic minor is "all white keys except B♭ and F♯"; D melodic minor is "all white keys except C♯" (p0092–0093).

### 2.5 Diminished and whole-tone scales: "repetition" from symmetry (p0096–0110)

| | Diminished scale | Whole-tone scale |
|---|---|---|
| Construction | Alternating half and whole steps, eight notes; **symmetrical**, so there are only three (p0096–0097) | Made entirely of whole steps; symmetrical, so there are only two (p0108) |
| Repetition | ★ **Everything can repeat in minor thirds**: licks, chords, and voicings alike; one voicing serves four V7♭9 chords (p0100–0102) | ★ **Everything can repeat in whole steps**, and therefore in major thirds and tritones; no avoid notes, so everything within the scale is interchangeable (p0108–0109) |
| Notation | | Used over G7♯5. Writing G7+ causes confusion (+ means a raised 5th), and writing G7♭13 is somewhat risky (it implies ♭9 ♯9, i.e. alt). **For safety, write G7♯5** (p0108) |
| Limits | | It lacks half of the intervals of Western music (minor 2nd, minor 3rd, perfect 4th, and so on), so it has a strong homogeneous sound and is best used in small doses. Only one or two of the 100 tunes in a typical fake book contain whole-tone chords (p0109). Many players simply use **alt in place of whole-tone chords** (bar 17 of "Stella": G7♯5 is played as G7alt, p0110) |

Quick guide to emotional colour (p0109): △7 is happy and calm; major triads are triumphant; melodic minor is dark, sad, and mysterious; dominant 7 is tense; diminished chords are extremely tense; the whole-tone scale stands apart as its own category.

### 3.1 Three ready-made recipes for expanding the circle of fifths (Valerio p0028–0033)

Static progressions need to be "expanded", and the way to do it is to fill in the circle of fifths moving counterclockwise. Three recipes you can copy directly:

| To connect | How to fill | Result (C major) |
|---|---|---|
| **I → V** | Along the fifth relationship A–D–G | Cmaj7 – Am7 – Dm7 – G7 |
| **I → II** | Walk four fifths starting from B7 | Cmaj7 – B7 – Em7 – A7 – Dm7 – G7 |
| **I → IV** | Walk seven fifths across four bars | C6 – Bm7♭5 – E7 – Am7 – D7 – Gm7 – C7 – F7 |

**Fifths and semitones are functionally equivalent**: a step down a fifth on the circle can be replaced by a semitone step down after tritone substitution (Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7 ↔ Dm7 – D♭7 – Cmaj7).
So the dominant 7 in any of the recipes above can be swapped for its tritone substitute, rewriting a chain of fifths as a bass line moving by semitones (p0033).
For passing and appoggiatura chords, see `mc-harmony` reference §2.8.

## 5. Family Tree of Dominant Function: Secondary, Extended, Substitute Dominants, and Related ii-7 (Chen Yunqiang, *Jazz Harmony*)

### 5.1 Secondary or extended dominant depends on where it falls in the harmonic rhythm

A dominant 7th chord with non-dominant function is **a secondary dominant when it sits on a weak position** (it resolves down a perfect fifth to a diatonic chord); **it is an extended dominant when it sits on a strong position** (a chain of dominant 7ths descending by perfect fifths, until a diatonic chord with non-dominant function appears) (around p0134).

| | Primary dominant 7 | Secondary dominant | Extended dominant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position | Mostly on weak positions | Weak position | **Strong position** |
| Root | 5th degree | Diatonic degrees | Mostly **6th, 3rd, 7th, 2nd** degrees; if the root is the 1st or 4th degree, it is treated as borrowed from the blues |
| Resolution | To any diatonic chord | Usually down a fifth; may **deceptively resolve** | Chained descending fifths |
| Tension | All six altered tensions | Mostly diatonic notes | |

(p0134, p0138, p0140) **Deceptive resolution**: a secondary dominant on a weak position does not resolve to a diatonic chord; instead it resolves to an extended dominant or another non-diatonic chord. It keeps its secondary-dominant label, with the label placed in brackets.
Set it side by side with Schoenberg's pseudo-cadence (`mc-harmony` reference §7.4) and Kachulis's false cadence (SKILL §3.1): three accounts of "the expected resolution that does not arrive".

### 5.2 Related ii-7: any dominant 7 can take one in front of it

**Any dominant 7 with dominant function may be preceded by a minor 7th chord whose root sits a perfect fifth below its root**, whether that chord is diatonic or not. When it is diatonic, it is called a related ii-7 with dual function (p0145–0147).
Each link in a chain of secondary dominants or extended dominants can have its related ii-7 inserted in front of it, and the chain remains a chain after insertion. This is the generating rule for "interlocking ii-V", and `mc-harmony` reference §3 only states that they "can be chained".

### 5.3 The scale of a secondary dominant is determined by its target chord

| Secondary dominant | Scale |
|---|---|
| V7/II, V7/III, V7/VI | Mixolydian ♭9 ♭13 (V7/II uses ♭13) |
| V7/IV, V7/V | Mixolydian |

★ **Mechanism**: the 4th is an avoid note in the scale of every secondary dominant, because it is the root of the target chord (p0126–0132). The altered scale list (♭9, ♭13, ♭9♯9, ♭9♯9♭13, Altered) marks the avoid notes for each entry; the Altered scale has none.
Tensions on substitute dominants and substitute related ii-7 chords can always use **9, ♯11, 13**, regardless of whether they are diatonic notes (p0225).

### 5.4 A primary dominant 7 does not always return to I

V7 can be redirected to: diatonic substitutes **III-7, VI-7**; or non-diatonic substitutes **♯IV°7, ♭IIImaj7, ♭VImaj7, ♭IImaj7** (the last four are borrowed from minor-mode conversions, and **their chord scale is always Lydian, with no avoid notes**).
Non-diatonic substitutes are only temporary transitions and still eventually return to I; **both the first substitute chord and the final I must sit on strong positions**. A ready-made chain: ♯IV°7 → IV-7 → III-7 (I/3 may stand in) → ♭III6 → ♭IImaj7 → I.

There are two types of chained substitute extended dominants: **alternating chains** (every other chord in the original chain is replaced by a substitute dominant, and the roots move down a minor second; determine this by checking whether the root of the first dominant chord is a diatonic note) and **wedge chains** (a substitute dominant is inserted between each pair of adjacent extended dominants, and both resolve to the next chord together).

### 5.5 Three categories of modulation (around p0250)

Direct modulation (no relationship, closely related keys, parallel modes); pivot-chord modulation (uses a diatonic chord or a chord from mode conversion as the pivot); dominant-chord modulation (treats the last dominant 7 of the original key as the primary, secondary, substitute, or non-dominant-function dominant 7 of the new key).
Compare with Huang Huwei (Huáng Hǔwēi)'s nine techniques in `mc-modulation` §3.5.
