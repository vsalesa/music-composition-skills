## 8. Drum Patterns and Tempo Quick Reference by Style (Drummer's Bible)

> How to use: first identify the **drum-pattern family + feel + tempo range** here, then return to `SKILL.md` §1.1 to follow the decision order, and finally fill in the ARR-SPEC `tempo` and `groove.feel`.
> Only copy tempos that the book states explicitly: ♩= quarter notes per minute, ♪= eighth notes per minute; "from ♩=x" means the book gives only a starting point.
> The "drum pattern" column records the division of kick / snare / cymbals in the book's main examples, not a bar-by-bar template.
> Where two books differ on a style, both are listed side by side, each with its source. Page numbers without a book title refer to the Drummer's Bible.

### 8.1 Rock Family

| Style | Drum pattern (kick / snare / cymbal) | Tempo | Feel | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early Rock 'n Roll | The main job is to deliver a solid feel to support dance music; keep everything simple | ♩=60–200 | Standard pattern works either way; **the band, not the drummer, decides straight or swung** | Ride pattern can be moved onto the snare (early rock only) | p0152–0154 |
| Rockabilly | Heavy snare backbeat (close to Jump Blues) | ♩=120–240 | swung | Train Beat becomes shuffled in this style; jazz swing pattern played on the ride | p0154 |
| Mersey Beat | Originated in Liverpool; heavily used by early rock and surf drummers | — | — | Permeates all of Surf | p0013, p0153, p0159 |
| Standard Rock | Basic is simple; **keep a strong 2 & 4 backbeat, with kick and snare variations built around it**; on-beat eighths louder than off-beat | ♩=60–200, most songs at 110–120 | straight | Hi-hat can move to the ride; stomping 2 & 4 with the foot is most practical; snare can be displaced; kick can add sixteenths; suits Classic/Pop Rock, New Wave, Soft Rock, Hard Rock, Grunge, Prog, etc. | p0021, p0155–0156 |
| Half-Time Standard Rock | Only for slow songs; fills the space at slow tempo | ♩=60–96 | halftime | Ride hand adds sixteenths to create a sense of liveliness | p0157 |
| Fusion | Instrumental fusion of Rock + Jazz + Funk + Prog, leaning mid-to-fast | Ghost-note groove ♩=136–172; 7/8 variant ♪=272–344 | straight, with many ghost notes | Sixteenth-note combinations between ride hand and snare, with snare playing ghosts; Linear Funk pattern is played much faster in Fusion than in Funk | p0157–0158 |
| Surf | Alternating-snare backbeat pattern; active repeated sixteenths on the toms (more common in instrumental pieces) | ♩=138–184; Wipeout tom pattern ♩=160 | straight | The eighth on the snare's 2nd off-beat can be omitted to simplify; Surf Ballad uses a Bolero pattern ♩=74–120 | p0159–0161 |
| Punk | Simple groove, almost always very loud; small, trashy kit | ♩=120–270, leaning fast | straight | Ride uses a large crash instead of a ride; foot on 2 & 4 is not always played; variants: half-open hi-hat standard rock / floor tom replacing cymbals / double-time / Mersey Beat | p0149–0150 |
| Heavy Metal | Standard rock pattern + loud half-open hi-hat, loud and aggressive throughout | ♩=100–200 (the style as a whole ranges from 60 to over 350) | straight | Two-Step/Polka pattern + decorative kick for double-time ♩=126–192; **Blast Beat**: hands alternate sixteenths between half-open hi-hat and snare, kick follows the ride hand, every note accented ♩=200; double kick: snare on 2 & 4 = half-time, snare moved to beat 3 = illusion of slowness, gallop foot pattern | p0105–0107, p0152 |
| Blues Rock / Straight Blues | Ride hand straight eighths; snare and kick also straight | ♩=80–160 | straight | In Blues, straight time is less common than swung, but both must be mastered | p0056–0057 |
| Latin Rock | Fits Afro-Cuban/Brazilian rhythms into the Rock format; pattern simplified to avoid duplicating percussionists, can be expanded again in solos | Standard rock songs ♩=110–120; double-time Latin feel ♩=200–232 | straight | Variants: Standard Rock + Cha Cha; Baiao (hi-hat alternating sixteenths, kick following hand accents); Mambo; Rumba | p0131–0133 |

Side by side: Groove Essentials divides Rock into three types — eighth-note groove, sixteenth-note groove, and half-time groove (p0019); four-on-the-floor is a "layered groove" in which every element must align perfectly with no flams, and it becomes muddy more easily at speed (p0022–0023); the backbeat of a half-speed groove falls on beat 3 (p0036).

### 8.2 Blues

| Style | Drum pattern | Tempo | Feel | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard Blues Shuffle | Ride hand plays the 1st and 3rd of each beat's triplet; on-beat louder than off-beat; strong snare 2 & 4 backbeat; kick "played with conviction" | ♩=80–160 | shuffle | The most common Blues groove | p0052–0053 |
| Chicago / Jazz Shuffle | Both hands in unison, louder; suits Blues or Big Band with horns; small bands use it for climaxes | ♩=80–160 | shuffle | Execution and timing of the unison hand pattern are the defining elements; can change to a variant every 8 bars | p0053–0054, p0118 |
| Backdoor (Upbeat) Shuffle | The only difference from Chicago: repeats the snare on the last triplet of each beat | ♩=80–160 | shuffle | Foot on 2 & 4, or all quarters | p0054 |
| 12/8 Slow Blues | Notated in 4/4, felt in 12/8, all 12 eighths played; snare 2 & 4 strong, kick 1 & 3 weak | ♩=50–80 | 12/8 | In soft passages, rim click replaces the backbeat; kick usually avoids 2 & 4 | p0055 |
| Purdie Shuffle | Standard Shuffle + snare ghost notes (second triplet of each beat, extremely soft, blended into the hi-hat) | ♩=100–160 | shuffle | Suggests half-time, often called Half-Time Purdie Shuffle | p0055–0056 |
| Blues Rumba | Four on the floor; variant with syncopated kick on the "+" of 1 and 2, and on 4 | ♩=108–160 | straight | Close to Cuban Bolero; open or closed resonant-head options both work | p0057–0058 |

Blues standard form is 12 bars, A-A-B lyric structure, swing time; straight eighths became common after rock emerged (p0051).

### 8.3 Funk

Funk drum patterns range from the simplest rock groove to complex rhythms; the feel ranges from relaxed, behind the beat, to tight, on top of the beat (p0094).

| Style | Drum pattern | Tempo | Feel | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New Orleans Funk | Repeated accents on the "3" side of clave + Second Line march feel; clearly defined cyclic rhythm | ♩=152–208 | in the crack (eighths relaxed and laid back) | Double sticks can replace the snare on closed hi-hat | p0095 |
| Displaced Funk | Main snare hits are not on 2 & 4; feel it in 4/4 (not double time) | ♩=100–138 | straight | **Keep at least one standard backbeat so the groove doesn't lose its footing**; displaced kick is also common in Funk | p0096–0097 |
| Linear Funk | No simultaneous sounds (no same-limb hits); lighter than layered type; usually sixteenths throughout | ♩=100–138 | straight | Snare must carry a backbeat somewhere in the bar | p0097–0098 |
| Ghost Note Funk | Layered: ghosts beneath the main voices, forming a subtle sixteenth feel around the backbeat | ♩=92–126 | straight | No standard pattern; ride hand may play the ride, foot on all quarters / 2 & 4 / all off-beats | p0098–0099 |
| Funk Rock | Fewer ghosts, stable backbeat, emphasis on displacing the kick from 1 & 3 | ♩=92–126 | straight | Kick displacement is almost unlimited (sixteenth combinations); often plays the last sixteenth of any beat with open hi-hat accents | p0099–0100 |

Side by side: Groove Essentials says Funk's signature is syncopated (shifted accents) (p0041); the main groove can avoid the beat-2 backbeat entirely (p0044); a "beat-2 displacement" type has a lopsided stutter feel (p0046); three checks for a busy groove — internal dynamic balance, accurate subdivision, ensemble awareness (p0043). For another account of linear drumming, see `SKILL.md` §4 (DeSantis).

### 8.4 R&B / Hip-Hop / Disco / Electronic

| Style | Drum pattern | Tempo | Feel | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hip Hop / Rap | No single standard pattern (a fusion of Rock + Funk); strong sense of time; small, high-pitched drums emulate a loop | Standard ♩=60–108; slow feels ♩=60–80; half-time feels ♩=132–172 | Straight or swung both work, **swung is more common** (sixteenths shuffled); **half-time is mostly straight** | Open hi-hat notes are a common colour | p0109–0111 |
| Acid Jazz | No standard pattern; relaxed groove + strong sense of time; often imitates drum machines | Hip Hop type ♩=60–120; Standard Rock ♩=110–120; Swing ♩=60–255; Bossa ♩=100–168; Mambo ♩=200; Displaced Funk ♩=100–138 | Depends on the borrowed style | Small kit, aiming for consistent timbre | p0021–0022 |
| Boogaloo | Salsa + R&B; an early Funk/R&B pattern that can be read as syncopated | ♩=88–120 | straight | See Displaced Funk | p0167 |
| Disco | Four on the floor; snare steady on 2 & 4; hi-hat straight sixteenths or open hi-hat on the off-beats | ♩=108–120 | straight | When on the ride, the most common is ride bell on the off-beats, foot on 2 & 4 or all on-beats | p0087–0088 |
| Techno (including House / Trance / Trip Hop / Breakbeat, etc.) | Simpler than D&B, demands more consistency; heavy four on the floor | ♩=110–184 | straight | Small high-pitched drums, trashy cymbals, or electronic drums | p0162 |
| Drum & Bass / Jungle | Pursues creative but repetitive loops; Jungle uses broken sixteenths, D&B is simpler/more atmospheric | ♩=144–192 | straight | Small high-pitched drums, trashy cymbals, triggering systems | p0089–0090 |

Side by side on whether hip-hop should be "loose": DeSantis says hip-hop suits a looser, more organic feel (*Making Music* p0154); the Drummer's Bible says swing is more common and half-time is mostly straight (p0109, p0111); Groove Essentials' hip-hop track is the **only** track in the book based on a loop with everything quantised to the grid, and the swing can be obvious or subtle (p0052); R&B sixteenth grooves use accents to push the focus toward eighths, and the common mistakes are losing subdivision clarity and playing too loudly (p0054); in the classic half-time shuffle the ride pattern determines the swing amount for the whole band (p0056). The three accounts do not really conflict: **the grid may be absolute; the swing amount and the dynamics of accents and ghosts are where the feel comes from.** The "third sense of time" in which straight and swung coexist belongs to `mc-style-hiphop` §3 and is not expanded here.

### 8.5 Jazz

| Style | Drum pattern | Tempo | Feel | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New Orleans Second Line | Triplet feel but played in the crack; kick projects 1 & 3, with a kick on beat 4 of the second bar to push forward; hi-hat foot on 2 & 4 answered with heel splash; snare eighths with varied accents | ♩=144–216 | in the crack | Improvisation-based, with countless variants of syncopation, accents and rolls; kick can lead into a 2-3 son clave | p0112–0113, p0135 |
| Dixieland | Jazz ride pattern on closed hi-hat; kick on 1 & 3; strong rim click on snare beat 4; foot on 2 & 4 | ♩=160–264 | swing | Ending: a two-bar fill, with a single splash hit on beat 4 of bar two, choked, no kick | p0114–0115 |
| Big Band | Time on the ride + hi-hat foot; ride 2 & 4 accents coinciding with the foot define the pocket | ♩=60–255 (can be higher) | swing | The "+" accents on ride 2 & 4 are tighter; in quiet passages time moves to the hi-hat; four on the floor played **lightly**, doubling the walking bass but not interfering with the bass line; in loud passages snare 2 & 4 backbeat | p0115–0117 |
| Big Band Figures | Ties or notes longer than a quarter = long notes: cymbal + kick (or cymbal + snare), with a strong set-up on the preceding beat; short notes: snare drummer, occasionally kick with choked hi-hat | Example piece ♩=134 | swing | Four set-up types: quarter note, drag, flam, four-stroke ruff | p0118–0120 |
| Small Band (Be Bop / Cool / Avant-garde) | Be Bop is busy, reaching ♩=300; Cool leaves space with few notes; Avant-garde time is often obscured or omitted, using more rhythmic complexity | ♩=60–304 (except Ballad) | swing (straight also appears after Fusion) | Time patterns can be mixed; the foot need not play 2 & 4 | p0121–0124 |
| Jazz Waltz | 3/4, almost always swung | ♩=100–255 | swing | Foot pattern most practical: 2 & 3 / only 2 / only 3 / the "+" of 2 | p0125 |
| Jazz Brushes / Ballad | Snare player draws circles clockwise, ride hand plays time on the same face; at slow tempos use a figure-eight pattern | Ballad from ♩=50; standard brush pattern works best at ♩=88 and above | swing | Kick very rarely plays all quarters, and must not overpower the brushes | p0126–0127 |

Side by side: Groove Essentials says swing is the root of jazz (p0059); whether notated as eighths, dotted, or triplets, everything is played with a triplet feel, and the triplets can be looser or tighter than notated (p0073, p0076); comping mainly comes from kick and snare (p0061); 2-feel reduces the rhythm section's intensity and volume, increases space, the commitment to time must not be reduced, and the bass plays half notes (p0074–0075); in waltz "in 3" the bass walks every beat, while in "in 1" the bass plays only beat 1 of each bar (p0076, p0078); swing gradually becomes straighter as tempo increases, a climb from ♩=75 to 350 (p0122).

### 8.6 Afro-Cuban

§8.6–8.8 are drum-pattern tables; which source to follow, how to set the clave, who keeps to the grid, and whether these can be moved elsewhere are decisions covered in `mc-style-latin`.

Prerequisite: the only difference between son clave and rumba clave is whether the last note on the "3" side falls on beat 4 or on the "+" of 4; there are four combinations of 3-2 / 2-3 (p0030); 6/8 clave is often notated as 4/4 triplets (p0031); when there are no percussionists, the drummer must replace the whole group alone (p0029).

| Style | Drum pattern | Tempo | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Afro-Cuban 6/8 (Bembe / Abakwa) | Ride hand plays the 6/8 bell pattern; kick on beat 1 and the last triplet of beat 4; snare player duplicates the conga melody between rim click and tom; foot on every on-beat | ♩=92–120 | Variant 1: snare accent on beat 3 = 6/8 backbeat; use the extra kick sparingly | p0032–0034 |
| Cascara | Ride hand plays cascara (closed hi-hat / floor tom shell / cowbell / ride bell); snare rim click; kick tumbao | ♩=126–250 | More authentic: kick plays only the bombo (the "+" of beat 2 on the "3" side) | p0034–0035 |
| Guaguanco | 3-2 rumba clave; ride hand cascara; kick emphasises bombo; snare conga pattern; foot 1 & 3 | ♩=200 | The cascara pattern can be substituted | p0036 |
| Mambo | 2-3 son clave over two bars; ride hand Mambo bell; snare duplicates conga; **kick supports the bass's tumbao, sometimes in unison and sometimes split**; foot 2 & 4 or 1 & 3 | ♩=200 | Bell accents played on the edge but not too loud | p0037 |
| Cha Cha | Half-time version of Mambo; ride hand cha bell continuing in quarters; snare/high tom conga pattern; kick on the "+" of 2 and beat 3 aligning with the bass; hi-hat doubling quarters or playing only 2 & 4 | From ♩=110 | Opening fill "Abanico": rim shot on beat 3, roll on beat 4 into beat 1 of the next bar | p0038–0039 |
| Mozambique | 2-3 rumba clave; ride hand Mozambique bell; snare plays conga-derived accents on snare / high tom / floor tom; foot and kick simple | From ♩=200 | Steve Gadd version: both feet play 1 & 3 in unison over two bars | p0039–0040 |
| Songo | The first Afro-Cuban style conceived directly from the drum kit; ride hand provides a steady pulse, kick plays a two-bar tumbao, snare fills syncopation around the other limbs as linear material | From about ♩=200 | Stomping the rumba clave with the foot will break a groove that has not yet settled | p0040–0041 |
| Bomba | Repeated kick pattern + distinctive bell pattern; usually no clave | From ♩=200 | Snare accent on the final note of every other bar | p0042 |
| Merengue | Floor tom repeating figure (originally conga); snare rim click throughout; kick four on the floor (often doubled by the foot) | From ♩=240 | Foot splash on beat 3 of two bars | p0042–0043 |
| Bolero | Slow and lyrical; the rhythm was originally played on claves/maracas, and the drummer duplicates it on the rim or a tightly closed hi-hat | ♩=74–120 | Also used for Surf Ballad, Hawaiian ballads | p0045, p0160 |
| Cumbia | A steady pattern of one quarter + two eighths; strong snare backbeat; **the repeating kick pattern usually matches the bassist**; foot doubles the backbeat; no clave | ♩=160–252 (double-time feel) | Similar to the Pilon pattern | p0047 |
| Danzon | Timbale predates the drum kit, so resonant heads are off; a detailed combination of rim click / finger strikes / stick strikes | ♩=110 | Interchangeable with Cha Cha and Bolero | p0048 |
| Palito / Pilon / Plena / Conga / Beguine | Palito ♩=120–264 (both hands between hi-hat and rim click, kick tumbao); Pilon ♩=172–224 (snare plays timbale melody, foot plays half notes); Plena ♩=208–276 (simple quarter-note kick, no clave); Conga ♩=216–264; Beguine ballroom ♩=112 to drum Beguine ♩=280 | — | — | p0044–0050 |

Side by side: Groove Essentials has Salsa's left hand play cross stick on the clave position and the right hand play cascara on cowbell (p0109); Songo's signature is that each bar's final sixteenth resolves cleanly (p0104); Mambo's rhythmic density is high, and the goal is "one voice" rather than stacking parts (p0102); when multiple percussionists are present, choose a variant with fewer notes to make room (p0100, p0103, p0109); the focus of Mozambique is the bell pattern, and if the tom interplay is unclear, play only the bell (p0108); Nañigo is a counting problem of 6 against 4 (p0106).

### 8.7 Brazilian

Do not confuse Brazilian with Afro-Cuban: each has its own rhythms (p0059).

| Style | Drum pattern | Tempo | Feel | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Samba | Foot (main kick) imitates surdo and cooperates with the bassist; hands circle around Partido Alto | From ♩=170 | Straight with a very slight swing, never settling in one place (Groove Essentials p0092) | — | p0059–0060 |
| Batucada | Busier than Samba, used for climaxes; ride hand duplicates surdo on the floor tom (including muted strokes); snare plays Partido Alto or tamborim pattern | From ♩=170 | — | When the hands are busy, the kick is simplified; two-bar sections are interchangeable | p0061–0062 |
| Baiao | The syncopated rhythm of zabumba; main pattern = first bar of the Brazilian clave; tempo close to Samba | From ♩=170 | In the variant, the hand's march-like pattern plays in the crack | Kick dotted eighth–sixteenth (Groove Essentials p0095: also the foundation of the Charleston and many hip-hop grooves) | p0062–0063 |
| Bossa Nova | Kick identical to Samba; ride hand steady eighths; snare rim click plays the "Brazilian clave" (variant Partido Alto) | ♩=100–168 | straight | Hi-hat eighths can be fully closed, or the foot pedal plays 2 & 4 to imitate the guiro | p0064 |

★ **When chord changes fall in Bossa** (Fujitani, p0104): on odd bars the chord changes on the downbeat, and **on even bars the chord changes half a beat early**; guitar and electric piano follow this, and **bass always changes on the downbeat**.
This rule almost defines the style: any song adjusted in this way acquires a bossa flavour. The standard lineup: acoustic drums, acoustic bass, **classical guitar (nylon strings, essential)**, piano or electric piano,
often with shaker, triangle, tamborim, or pandeiro (p0104–0108); the example piece is at BPM 120 (p0108).

Side by side: Groove Essentials says in Samba all elements have equal volume, and the hi-hat imitates pandeiro, "loose but not muddy" (p0092); in fast Samba the kick is light and flows forward, and should not be stepped on by other instruments (p0094); in Batucada the snare is the focus and the floor tom = surdo, pursuing overall flavour rather than exact notes (p0097); Bossa is light-touch and leaves space, and the double-time hint should be used with great sensitivity (p0091); Merengue is the most energetic groove, and when sharing the stage with percussionists it should actively reduce its density (p0099).

### 8.9 Country

General: simple and steady; snare rim click is very common, and brushes often play the ride pattern directly on the snare (p0077).

| Style | Drum pattern | Tempo | Feel | Signature treatment | Page |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bluegrass Train Beat | Snare sustained sixteenths with accents on the off-beats, imitating fiddle/banjo; brushes | ♩=100–160, cut-time feel | straight | Variant: jazz swing pattern in sixteenth notation ♩=60–120, fast enough to be nearly straight | p0078–0079 |
| Western Swing | Big Band swing pattern but using rim click (more refined) | ♩=132–216, ballads slower | swing | In soft passages the ride hand uses closed hi-hat or brushes; ride + foot + full snare are reserved for loud passages | p0079–0080 |
| Country Two-Beat | Two feel, emphasising off-beats; = Polka pattern with rim click replacing the snare | ♩=80–132 | straight | Variant: ride hand emphasises off-beats + snare 2 & 4 | p0080–0081 |
| Country Shuffle | Ride hand plays all eighths in shuffle; swing eighths borrowed from Jazz/Blues | ♩=80–126 | shuffle | **Most shuffle patterns are too busy for traditional country**; reserved for Country Rock / modern country | p0081–0082 |
| Western | = slow version of Country Shuffle + rim click; ride is almost never used | ♩=80–92 | shuffle (slow) | Variants are extremely rare | p0082 |
| Train Beat (Country) | Same as Bluegrass but slower, felt in 4/4; sticks can be used | ♩=100–132 | straight | Many snare notes emulate double-time | p0083 |
| Country Rock / Modern Day Country | Standard Rock pattern; Country Rock is more active with a bigger drum sound; modern country relies on pop grooves and loops | ♩=58 (or slower) to over 184; aggressive variant ♩=92–152 | straight | — | p0083–0084 |
| Country Waltz | **Snare rarely on beat 2** (distinguishes it from Jazz / Cajun / American waltz) | ♩=76–116 | usually swung, rarely straight | Busy variants used for climaxes | p0084–0085 |
| Country Ballad | 12/8 pattern + rim click; Country Rock Ballad; Half-Time; Half-Time Waltz | 12/8 ♩=50–80; rock ballad ♩=60–72; half-time ♩=60–72; half-time waltz ♩=76–100 | — | — | p0085–0086 |

Side by side: Groove Essentials says the classic 2-beat works from Hard Rock to Light Broadway (p0083); a fast country two can use a snare "chicken pickin'" pattern as a substitute (p0084).

### 8.11 Fills and Ending Rules by Style (p0172–0183)

- A fill is a transitional figure that must blend seamlessly into the groove, with stability matching that of the groove (p0172); standard fills are rare, but **the type of fill varies by style** (p0172); the order of variation: note value → accent → syncopation → orchestration (p0172); fills longer than two bars are usually impractical (p0185).
- **Endings**: in most styles the fill ends on beat 1 of the next bar; **4/4 Afro-Cuban** ends on beat 4 of the fill bar (ponche), with the groove returning on beat 2 of the next bar; **Jazz** ends on the "+" of beat 4, with the groove likewise returning on beat 2 (p0181).
- Applicable note values (p0181–0183):

| Style | Applicable fills |
|---|---|
| Standard Rock / Hip Hop / Disco / Linear Funk / Odd Time / Cha Cha | All |
| Displaced & Ghost Note Funk / Funk Rock / Straight Blues Rock / Country Rock / Bluegrass | All, though triplets are rare in straight time |
| Shuffle types / Reggae / Western Swing / Country Shuffle / Rockabilly | Swung eighths, eighth triplets (and their off-beats) |
| 12/8 types | Swung eighths, triplets (in 12/8 triplets are notated as eighths) |
