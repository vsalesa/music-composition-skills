# Latin Style Layer (Latin)

**The three pillars of this style layer: choose the right family first, let the clave act as the grammar, and hand the grid to the bells rather than the drums.**

★ **"Latin" is not one style. It is three families that do not intermarry.**
*The Drummer's Bible* makes this explicit at the opening of its Brazil chapter: **do not confuse Brazilian with Afro-Cuban. The two have their own characteristics and their own specific rhythms. When a song is labelled "Latin", first find out which kind of rhythm pattern it calls for** (p0059).
This skill makes that separation decision; the specific drum patterns are not covered here.

## 2. ★ Transfer table

| Device | Can it be moved out? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Baiao's dotted eighth plus sixteenth bass drum** | ✅ **Fully portable, and already moved** | It is also the basis of the Charleston, and of a large number of hip-hop grooves (Groove Essentials p0095). Once moved, nobody hears it as Brazilian |
| **Brazilian "second half of beat 4 anticipated"** | ✅ Portable | Anticipation on the "+" of beat 4 is a common Brazilian trait (Jazz Guitar Groove p0037). Essentially anticipation, which the general layer already covers (`mc-rhythm-groove` §3) |
| **Bossa's chord-change timing** | ✅ ★ **Portable, and the most valuable** | Odd-numbered bars change on the downbeat; even-numbered bars change half a beat early (Fujitani p0104). This is nearly a plug-and-play adaptation switch: run any song through it and it takes on a bossa flavour |
| **"The last sixteenth of each bar lands clean"** | ✅ Portable | The signature of songo (Groove Essentials p0104). The book states directly that this is an important trait of sixteenth-note music in general, not specific to Latin |
| **Conga / tumbao two-bar loop** | ⚠️ Half-portable | Can be moved as a percussion bed. Once a clave and bell are added, the whole passage is heard as Afro-Cuban |
| **Cascara / palito figure** | ⚠️ Half-portable | It is the thing that "completes the other side of the clave" (p0034, p0048). Detached from the clave, it is only a syncopated sixteenth line |
| **Cha cha bell's sustained quarter notes** | ❌ **Essentially not portable** | An extremely strong style marker; as soon as it sounds, it is cha cha (p0038) |
| **Mambo bell / mozambique bell** | ❌ Not portable | The bell pattern itself is the identity card of these two branches (p0037, p0108) |
| **The clave itself** | ❌ ★ **Not portable** | It is not a rhythmic embellishment. It is a grammar that governs the direction of every part (§3). Pasting a clave into a non-Latin song only produces a two-bar loop pulling against itself |
| **One drop (rim and bass drum together on beat 3)** | ❌ Not portable | As soon as it sounds, it is reggae (p0074) |
| **The discipline of "reduce when sharing the stage with a percussionist"** | ✅ ★ **Portable, and should be** | See §4.3. This is the most valuable thing Latin music can offer general arrangement |

★ **One test:**
> **Can it be moved? Check whether it depends on the clave.**
> Those that depend on the clave (bell patterns, cascara, the directional feel of tumbao) become meaningless when moved out.
> Those that do not depend on the clave (the dotted bass drum shape, the timing of anticipation, the discipline of reduction) can be moved freely.

## 3. ★ Clave: the grammar of this layer

### 3.1 What it is

In Spanish, "clave" means "key", as in the key of a piano, not the llave that opens a lock (p0030).
By definition it is a **two-bar rhythm pattern**, divided into a "3" side with three notes and a "2" side with two notes.
**You can start from either side**, which is why there are 3-2 and 2-3 arrangements. It is the foundation of most Afro-Cuban rhythms (p0012).

★ **What it means when writing an arrangement:** the clave is not an optional percussion track.
**It is the grammar that decides which way all the other two-bar figures face.**

### 3.2 Son or rumba

| | Son clave | Rumba clave |
|---|---|---|
| **The last note of the "3" side falls on** | ★ **Beat 4** | ★ **The "+" of beat 4** |
| **The only difference** | Just the line above (p0030) | |
| **Mostly found in** | More traditional branches: mambo, cha cha (p0013) | More modern branches: songo, mozambique (p0013) |

**Test:** first identify the branch, and the clave type follows. There is no need to guess.
Guaguanco is 3-2 rumba (p0036); mambo is 2-3 son (p0037);
mozambique and songo are both 2-3 rumba (p0039, p0040); pilon is based on 2-3 son (p0049).

### 3.3 2-3 or 3-2

**Test:** see which side the ensemble's phrase starts on.
Mambo can start from either the "2" side or the "3" side (p0037); guaguanco usually starts from the "3" side (p0036).
So this is not a musicological question. It is a **notation question about the starting point of a phrase**:
write out the melody and the ensemble figure first, then decide which side of the clave to line up with the first bar.

★ **Once decided, all two-bar figures flip together.**
The position of the cascara and palito notes relative to the clave is fixed,
**so their relationship to the clave is unchanged whether 3-2 or 2-3** (p0034, p0048).
When you flip the clave, you cannot flip only the clave track; the whole group of two-bar figures flips together.

### 3.4 What "following the clave" means

Three checkable landing points, to be used as validation points while writing:

| Name | Position | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **bombo** | ★ The **"+" of beat 2** on the "3" side of the clave | The most authentic landing point for the bass drum. To sound more Cuban, reduce the bass drum to only this stroke (p0035); guaguanco also emphasises this (p0036) |
| **ponche** | The accent on **beat 4** of a 4/4 bar | A transitional figure (p0013) |
| **The last sixteenth of each bar** | Lands clean | The signature of songo, and a general rule for sixteenth-note music (Groove Essentials p0104) |

**Test (following):** for every fixed two-bar figure, its accent should land on the same side as the clave.
**Test (clashing):** the figure's accent lands on the opposite side.
The typical symptom is that the **"+" of the bombo falls into the bar that has only two notes**,
so the low-frequency centre of gravity runs against the clave's three-note side, and the whole thing sounds twisted.
> ⚠ The Latin community calls this twisted feel *cruzado*. **This term is not covered by the source text.** The test above is inferred backwards from the principle that the palito/cascara relationship to the clave is constant (p0034, p0048).

### 3.5 When not to write a clave

- **Songo with a rumba clave underfoot:** adding it before the groove is firmly established interrupts the overall feel (p0040).
  ★ This generalises: **the clave is added on top of stability; it is not used to establish stability.**
- **Branches that have no clave to begin with:** bomba (p0042), cumbia (p0047) and plena (p0050) usually do not play a clave; do not force one on them.
- **Pilon:** has a clave as its base, but is usually not played by the drummer (p0049).

### 3.6 The 6/8 branch

Afro-Cuban 6/8 is based on a feel of triple metre. It originates with the Yoruba people of Nigeria and in Cuba is often called Bembe or Abakwa (p0013, p0031).
**It has its own clave and its own bell pattern** (p0012). The 6/8 bell pattern is identical to the 6/8 clave,
with two additional notes, and those two notes should be played more softly than the others (p0032).

★ **Notation decision:** to convert between this and the 4/4 sections of a song, notate it as 4/4 triplet groupings.
**The book says the purpose of this is precisely to establish its relationship to the 4/4 Cuban clave** (p0031).
To have performers phrase in three, notate it in 6/8.
Counting is the main difficulty of this branch; Groove Essentials calls Nañigo directly "a six-against-four counting problem" (p0106).

## 4. ★ Division of labour in the groove: who holds what

### 4.1 Four roles

| Role | What it holds | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Conga tumbao** | The standard, repeated conga figure, mainly landing on the high drum, the quinto | p0013 |
| **Bass tumbao** | ★ **The bass's main figure in Afro-Cuban music**; when there is a drum kit, the bass drum usually doubles or reinforces it with accents | p0013 |
| **Piano montuno** | A repeated, syncopated piano figure | p0013 |
| **Timbale cascara** | A two-bar shell figure on the timbale, **completing the other side of the clave**; derived from the palito, originally played on a hollowed bamboo gua gua | p0012, p0048 |
| **Bell** | ★ **Sustained pulse; it is the bell that holds the grid** | §4.2 |

### 4.2 ★ Who holds the grid

**In Latin music, the grid is held not by the drums but by the bell and the feet.**

- The signature of cha cha is the **unbroken quarter notes on the cha bell**, which the hi-hat often doubles (p0038)
- In 6/8, the hi-hat foot **presses on every downbeat** (p0033)
- Merengue, soca and ska use a four-on-the-floor bass drum as the pulse (p0042, p0072, p0073)
- The strong identity of plena is **a simple quarter-note bass drum** (p0050)

★ **Why this matters:** the clave is two-bar, syncopated, and does not land on the grid.
**If nothing sounds steadily on the downbeats, the clave has no point of reference, and it just sounds like a string of wrong notes.**
This is the same principle as the rule in `mc-style-citypop-rnb` §4.1, that something must hold the grid for the push-and-pull to work, seen from another angle.

### 4.3 The discipline of reducing alongside percussionists

The book says the same thing repeatedly; it is worth recording as a rule:

| Situation | Approach | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| No percussionist | The drum kit must cover the jobs of conguero, timbalero and bongo bell player, which demands very high independence | p0029 |
| One percussionist | Choose the variant with fewer notes (cha cha, mambo and merengue all give a simplified version to play "with the conga player") | p0038, p0043, p0100 |
| Several percussionists | ★ **Reduce proactively and step aside.** Merengue states it most plainly: gently reduce your own contribution, and do not step on others' parts | p0099, p0109 |
| Complex interplay you cannot sort out | **Play only the bell pattern.** Mozambique's point is the bell anyway; if the tom interplay gets tangled, drop it first | p0108 |

★ **These four rules can be transplanted wholesale into any arrangement: the denser the lineup, the less each person writes.**

### 4.4 How the source handles rushing and pulling

- **The Brazilian branch has clear anticipation:** anticipation on the "+" of beat 4 is a common trait of Brazilian music (Jazz Guitar Groove p0037).
- **In bossa, what is rushed is the harmony instrument, not the bass:** guitar and electric piano change chords half a beat early in even-numbered bars,
  **while the bass always changes on the downbeat** (Fujitani p0104).
- **In Afro-Cuban music, bass drum and bass are two faces of the same line:** the bass drum supports the bass tumbao, **sometimes playing in unison, sometimes splitting** (p0037);
  the bass drum repeat pattern in cumbia usually matches the bass player directly (p0047); in samba, the feet imitate the surdo while coordinating with the bass player (p0059).

> ⚠ **The claim that "in salsa, the bass always rushes ahead of the previous chord" is not covered by the source.**
> The three points above are all the source supports. When filling `groove.push_pull`, use these three points and do not fill in millisecond values without a traceable source.

★ **One usable rule:** `arrangement.bass_kick_relation` in Afro-Cuban music **defaults to bound** (the bass drum doubles the bass tumbao with accents, p0013), which is the opposite of the interlocking approach used in city pop.

## 5. The Brazilian branch

### 5.1 The surdo is the underlying logic

On the drum kit, the foot part of the Brazilian branch (mainly the bass drum) **imitates the surdo, the large drum**, while coordinating with the bass player (p0059).
Batucada goes further: **the ride hand moves to the floor tom to replicate the surdo's sound, including the muted stroke** (p0061, Groove Essentials p0097).

★ **How to read this for arrangement:** **the low end of the Brazilian branch is not a "bass drum part"; it is a large drum broken up onto the kit.**
So when writing Brazilian low end, first ask "which stroke of the surdo is this?", and then ask which beat it lands on.

### 5.2 Partido alto

Partido alto is both one of the most popular samba branches in Brazil and a rhythm pattern used in both samba and bossa.
Traditionally it is played on agogo, cuica, pandeiro and tamborim; on the drum kit it usually falls to the snare (p0013).
Samba's hands revolve around it (p0059), batucada's snare plays it (p0061), and one of bossa's rim-click variants is also this pattern (p0064).

★ **Test:** **the same partido alto, played by different instruments and at different dynamics, across these three branches, changes the style.**
The figure itself is not the style marker; **the instrument and dynamic carrying it are.**

### 5.3 Where the lightness of bossa comes from

Three traceable sources; all three must be in place for it to work:

| Source | Specifics | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Drum timbre and playing technique** | ★ **Restrained drum-kit timbre, avoiding flashy rock kits; closed rim-click playing is the mainstream approach, so choose drums whose rim sound is pleasant** | Fujitani p0105 |
| **Touch and space** | ★ **Light touch, leaving space**; be extremely sensitive about hinting at double-time | Groove Essentials p0091 |
| **Timing of chord changes** | Odd bars change on the downbeat, even bars change half a beat early (guitar and electric piano follow this); **the bass always changes on the downbeat** | Fujitani p0104 |

**Two positions side by side:** *The Drummer's Bible* says the bass drum in bossa is **identical to that in samba**;
the only differences are a slower, more relaxed tempo and harmony borrowed from American jazz (p0063, p0064). Fujitani, by contrast, focuses on instrumentation and the timing of chord changes (p0102–0108).
★ The two positions do not conflict. Combined, they give: **bossa ≈ samba skeleton + jazz harmony + a full set of disciplined reduction in execution.**

### 5.4 Why samba never settles

The feel of samba is **straight with a very slight sway, never settling into any one place.** The band must be "loose without turning to mush"; these two are very different.
Each instrument is at equal volume, and the hi-hat imitates the pandeiro (Groove Essentials p0092).
In fast samba, the bass drum should be light and flow forward, not stomp on other instruments (p0094).

**Two positions side by side:** Jazz Guitar Groove says the eighth notes in samba and bossa are **closer to straight** (p0040);
Groove Essentials describes it as liquid, somewhere between straight and a slight sway, never settling (p0092).
★ When writing `groove.feel`: **enter `straight16`, and write in `quantize_policy` that it is "close to straight, with very slight non-settling allowed"**;
do not use a swing ratio to express it.

### 5.5 Baiao

The main percussion instrument is the zabumba, a large bass drum, played syncopated on both sides (p0062).
The main pattern is the same as the first bar of the Brazilian clave, and the tempo is closer to samba than to bossa (p0062).
The bass drum is the classic **dotted eighth followed by a sixteenth** (Groove Essentials p0095).
It is not a fusion of bossa and samba, but it has the rhythmic elements of both (p0062).

★ **This dotted figure is the most portable thing in this skill** (§2): it is the basis of the Charleston and of many hip-hop grooves (p0095).

## 6. Lineup and register discipline

### 6.1 The bossa lineup has essential parts

| Part | Level of necessity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Classical guitar (nylon strings)** | ★ **Essential** | Fujitani says the nylon guitar is absolutely the main instrument of bossa; **guitar and vocals alone are enough to perform bossa** (p0104, p0107). The three high strings are nylon, which is the source of the sweet, soft tone (p0107) |
| Acoustic drum kit | Standard lineup | Restrained timbre; the rim sound should be good (p0105) |
| **Acoustic bass** | Use it for authentic bossa | An electric bass is acceptable if you want a bit of pop feel (p0106). ★ **This is an explicitly stated tier choice** |
| Piano or electric piano | Standard lineup | Electric pianos are mostly Fender Rhodes-type; tremolo, chorus and phaser are common (p0107) |
| Percussion | Often added | Shaker, triangle, tamborim (six-inch single-headed frame drum), pandeiro (ten-inch frame drum with jingles) (p0108) |

The demonstration piece's tempo is BPM 120 (p0108).

### 6.2 The Afro-Cuban lineup

- **A three-person percussion group is the default:** conguero, timbalero, bongo bell player (p0029)
- **Mambo's historical recipe:** the instrumentation, improvisation and harmonic setup of American swing, plus the rhythms of Cuban son (p0036).
  ★ So brass in mambo sections has a documented origin

The traditional instrument lists for each branch (the Danzon's bowed strings, plena, merengue, calypso, etc.) are in [reference.md](reference.md) §4.

### 6.3 A roster recommendation

★ **The frequency positions below are relative positions based on the nature of each instrument. They have not been measured in this library, and no measured frequency values can be cited.**

| Part | Role | Frequency position (relative) | Suggestion for entering/exiting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **surdo / zabumba / bombo** | Low-frequency pulse | Lowest | Throughout the Brazilian and baiao sections; if it leaves, the branch's identity goes with it |
| **Acoustic bass** | Low-frequency melody | Low | Throughout |
| **Conga (tumbao)** | Mid-low hand-drum loop | Mid-low | Can be removed for a whole section as a subtraction |
| **Timbale / tom cascara and timbale melody** | Mid-range syncopated line | Mid | ★ **Clearer to choose between this and the bell** (§6.4) |
| **Cowbell / agogo / cha bell** | ★ **Sustained pulse that holds the grid** | Mid-high | To contrast sections, remove it entirely |
| **Clave / rim click / palito / wood block** | Audible form of the grammar | ★ **Mid-high woody point; the most crowded slot in the whole piece** | §6.4 |
| **Shaker / cabasa / guiro / triangle / pandeiro** | High-frequency continuous feel | High | Competes with the hi-hat for the same slot |
| **Nylon-string guitar (bossa)** | Harmony plus rhythm | Mid | ★ An essential part of bossa; it cannot be pulled out midway |
| **Piano / electric piano (montuno)** | Syncopated harmonic loop | Mid | The montuno section is the most natural entry point (§7.1) |

### 6.4 ★ Register discipline: in Latin music, the mid-high woody hits are the easiest to muddy

**The clave, rim click, palito, wood block, and the sound of cascara struck on the drum shell all crowd into the same narrow mid-high slot.**
This is the most distinctive congestion point of this layer. Other styles crowd the low-mid band (→ `mc-texture-layering`); here it is a **percussive transient point**.

There are three ready-made solutions, all of which come down to writing less rather than fixing in post-production:

1. **Remove parts:** when there are several percussionists, each reduces proactively (p0099, p0103, p0109)
2. **Keep only the backbone:** if the interplay is unclear, play only the bell pattern (p0108)
3. **Change the surface:** the cascara can be played on closed hi-hat, the shell or hoop of a floor tom, cowbell, or ride bell; choose one (p0034),
   ★ **choosing it is choosing the frequency band**

★ At the general layer these three map onto the three options in `mc-texture-layering` §4.2: "separate the register / let them share frequencies / alternate them".
**In this Latin layer, the choice is almost always "remove parts".**

## 7. Harmony

**General harmony is not covered here at all:** for sevenths, tension notes, ii-V, secondary dominants and slash chords, see `mc-harmony`; for the jazz harmony body that bossa borrowed, see `mc-style-jazz` §3.
This section covers only the two items specific to these three families.

### 7.1 Montuno: both a figure and a section

| Meaning | Notes |
|---|---|
| **① A figure** | A **repeated, syncopated** pattern, usually played on piano (p0013) |
| **② A section** | ★ **The section in Afro-Cuban arrangement that supports improvisation** (p0013) |

**Historical corroboration:** the true Danzon had a fixed form (paseo, flute theme, repeated introduction, violin section); when son arrived, a fourth part was added, originally called "Nuevo Ritmo", later known as mambo. **This is where improvisation was introduced** (p0048).

★ **Two arrangement tests you can use directly:**
1. **The montuno section is the improvisational exit in the form.** To leave space for a solo, place it in the montuno section,
   rather than carving holes into the theme section (for general form theory, see `mc-form`).
2. **The harmony of montuno must be a short loop.** A repeated syncopated piano figure only works if the harmony also repeats;
   forward motion in the section is provided by density and entries/exits, not by chords.

> ⚠ **Which specific chords form the loop is not covered by the source.** The source gives the nature of the figure and the function of the section, but not the chord numbers for salsa.
> For specific loops, take them from `mc-progressions`, and write the numbers clearly in `form[].harmony`.

### 7.2 Bossa harmony

The view of the Fujitani branch (p0103–0104):

- As in jazz, **four-note voicings, extended chords and slash chords are used extensively** (p0103)
- ★ **The distance between the melody and the chord root is often very large, and this is where the distinctive floating feel comes from** (p0103)
- The **C△7(9)** that opens the piece is an extremely common bossa chord. The melody often rests on the fifth; adding M7 and 9 makes it more stylish than a plain C (p0103)
- To vary a bar, replace C with **C/E** (third in the bass) for a floating feel (p0104)
- The ending **D♭△7(9) → C△7(9)**: the melody rests on the most stable Do, but the chord above turns that Do into an M7, then a semitone descent leads back to the tonic chord to close (p0104)

The view from *The Drummer's Bible* (p0063): bossa's composers were mostly from the upper-middle class,
**borrowing the melodic character and chord structure of American jazz, but keeping samba's drum rhythms**, at a slower and more relaxed tempo.
The two sources both say the harmony comes from jazz.
> ⚠ **The claim that "bossa has lower harmonic density than jazz" is not covered by the source.**
> What the source supports is "slower and more relaxed" (p0063) and "four-note voicings, extended chords and slash chords used as usual" (p0103).
> If you want lower density, that is an arrangement decision: write it in the note at `form[].harmony`, and do not treat it as a definition of the style.

### 7.3 Chord-change timing is part of the harmony

★ The bossa rule, **odd bars change on the downbeat, even bars change half a beat early, and the bass always changes on the downbeat** (Fujitani p0104),
spans two layers in this library's conventions: it is both harmonic rhythm and groove.
When writing it, cover both: write the chords at `form[].harmony`, and on the `groove` side write "the harmony instrument anticipates by half a beat, the bass does not anticipate".

## 8. ★ The division between the three families (plus one)

| | **Afro-Cuban** | **Brazilian** | **Caribbean** | **Latin rock** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Representative branches** | son-salsa, mambo, cha cha, songo, mozambique, guaguanco, 6-8 bembe | samba, batucada, bossa, baiao | calypso, soca, ska, reggae | The rhythms of the three families above, placed into a rock form |
| **Has a clave?** | ★ **Yes, and it is the grammar** (son/rumba × 2-3/3-2) | There is a notion of a "Brazilian clave", used in bossa and baiao (p0064, p0062) | ★ **Essentially no clave** | Depends on which branch is borrowed |
| **Role of low end** | Bass drum doubling the bass tumbao (p0013, p0037) | ★ **Bass drum imitating the surdo** (p0059) | Four on the floor (soca, ska) or the one drop on beat 3 (reggae) | Simplified standard rock bass drum (p0131) |
| **What holds the grid** | Sustained pulse of the bell | Continuous sixteenths in the hands (imitating the pandeiro, p0092) | Bass drum | Standard rock backbeat |
| **Feel** | Straight | ★ **Between straight and a slight sway, never settling** (p0092) | Ska straight; reggae sits in the crack (p0073, p0074) | Straight |
| **Typical tempo** | Medium to very fast; see reference | Fast; samba starts from ♩=170 (p0059) | Medium-slow to medium | ♩=110–120, up to double-time at 200–232 (p0131) |

★ **The reason these cannot be mixed is stated explicitly in the book** (p0059). Mixing them results in two two-bar grammars pulling against each other;
the low-frequency centre of gravity no longer lines up with the bell's pulse, and it sounds neither Cuban nor Brazilian.

### 8.1 Two distinctions within the Caribbean

| Pair | Where the difference lies | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Ska vs reggae** | Both play a rim click on beat 3; ★ **Ska is straight feel plus four on the floor, and that is the difference** | p0073 |
| **Reggae's one drop** | The rim click on beat 3 and the bass drum are struck **together as a single note**, which is where the name comes from | p0074 |
| **Calypso vs soca** | Calypso's bass drum is more syncopated and has no sustained snare pattern, so its groove is less pronounced than soca's; soca is faster, with a heavier four on the floor plus a prominent syncopated snare | p0071, p0072 |
| **Ska guitar** | ★ **Strongly and evenly emphasises all the offbeats**; this is the main source of the iconic sound, and guitarists call it "reverse accompaniment" | p0073 |

★ **Calypso has one execution detail worth noting separately:** **the snare wires must be switched off, and the crossstick sound is made by striking the snare shell with the hand; otherwise it sounds like a marching band rather than a Caribbean rhythm section** (Groove Essentials p0089).

### 8.2 Latin rock is an explicit "lower-authenticity" tier

- Definition: fusing Afro-Cuban and Brazilian rhythms into a rock form, often using percussion parts from all three families (p0131)
- ★ The drum kit patterns it uses are **less authentic than the two authentic chapters, and usually simplified** (p0131). This is the book's positioning, and it is not pejorative.
  Simplification avoids duplicating other percussionists; when played alone, the kit can be expanded again to approach a more authentic sound (p0131)
- Tempo: from standard rock's ♩=110–120, up to double-time Afro-Cuban / Brazilian-feel ♩=200–232 (p0131)
- Ready-made recipes: standard rock plus cha cha (p0131); baiao plus a rock feel (p0132); mambo but with the bass drum changed to emphasise beat 1 of two-bar units (p0133)

★ **How to read this for pop arrangement:** **if you want a "Latin flavour" without taking on the authentic division of labour, the Latin rock branch is the legitimate tier for you.**
Choose it, and state in `meta` that this is what you chose. That is far more honest than writing a vague "latin".

## 10. Checklist after writing

- [ ] State **which family and which branch**; no vague "latin" appears
- [ ] ★ **Brazilian and Afro-Cuban figures are not mixed in the same passage** (p0059)
- [ ] Clave type and starting side are set, **with the reason being the branch, not an arbitrary choice**
- [ ] ★ All fixed two-bar figures **face the same side**, and the bombo lands on the "+" of beat 2 on the "3" side (§3.4)
- [ ] Branches that have no clave to begin with (bomba, cumbia, plena) **have not been given a forced clave**
- [ ] ★ **Something sustained on the downbeats holds the grid** (bell or four on the floor), so the clave has a point of reference
- [ ] The number and division of percussion parts match (reduce to the number of people you have, §4.3)
- [ ] Mid-high woody hits: **only the necessary ones are kept**, and the solution is stated as remove parts / keep only the backbone / change the surface
- [ ] Bossa: nylon-string guitar is present, drums use rim clicks, **harmony instrument changes half a beat early while the bass changes on the downbeat**
- [ ] Samba: the low end is written as surdo strokes
- [ ] The montuno section's harmony is a **short loop**, and it carries either improvisation or density-driven forward motion
- [ ] ★ When transplanting to another style, §2 was checked: **no clave-dependent element was moved out**
- [ ] When only "Latin flavour" is wanted, the Latin rock tier was explicitly chosen and written into `meta` (§8.2)
