## 5. Section Structure, Ad-libs, and Vocal Sound Effects

### 5.1 Structural Numbers

| Unit | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Verse** | Standard **16 bars** ("a 16"); the formula is three 16s. Shorter suits a less core audience (they remember only the hook). Rampage writes 12 bars because 16 or 24 is too long. **Story rap caps at 32**; a showcase single verse has no upper limit (Kane once wrote 108 bars) | *How to Rap* p0206–0209 |
| **Hardest bars in a verse** | Big Pooh: **bars 1–2, 9–10, and 15–16**, which are the opening, middle, and close of the section | *How to Rap* p0210 |
| **Hook** | Usually **4 or 8 bars**; the shorter, the easier to remember. Simpler words than the verse, lower density, **words stretched a bit longer**. A minimal hook can be a single line. **Melodic hooks are more popular than spoken-rap hooks.** Hooks are not used in two cases: when the point is to show off wordplay, and in story songs where a hook would interrupt the plot | *How to Rap* p0200–0202 |
| **Who supplies the hook** | A hook preset by the producer **determines the content direction of the whole song**. Pharrell gives the **cadence**, and the MC fills words into that cadence | *How to Rap* p0246, p0248–0249 |
| **Hook melody** | The first phrase rises like a "question," the second falls like an "answer." Even when the words are not a question and answer, the feel is there. A melody ending on a rising pitch feels "unanswered" | *How to Rap 2* p0162–0164 |
| **Back-and-forth vocals** | Several MCs alternate every few words or every line (the early-hip-hop routine, comparable to harmony in doo-wop). Two-bar parts can be written separately and then assembled; word-by-word interleaving is usually written by one person and then divided up. You must know where the other person starts and where they end. A **posse cut** has four or five or more MCs back to back, with each one starting as soon as the previous one ends (p0234) | *How to Rap* p0230–0234 |
| **Switching flow between sections** | Separate with rests, rhyme-scheme changes, or **sudden changes in rhyme density** (§3.4, §3.5) | *How to Rap 2* p0203 |

### 5.2 Ad-libs, Stacking, and Hype Men

| Item | Content | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Stacking the whole section** | Makes the words fuller | *How to Rap* p0295 |
| **Stacking only keywords** | More common; Twista doubles only **the last word of each bar** | *How to Rap* p0295 |
| **Extreme layering** | Cashis: 11 vocal tracks on the hook, 8 on the verse, and 2 ad-lib tracks throughout | *How to Rap* p0295 |
| **Definition of ad-lib** | Improvised comments mixed into the background | *How to Rap* p0295 |
| **Hype man** | Provides live backing vocals and ad-libs on stage and gets the audience going (Flavor Flav). Royce's flow was so dense that someone had to fill in when he ran out of breath. Lateef: by about the **fifth** track of a set, you need to arrange for a DJ or harmony to come in on a line and let the MC catch his breath | *How to Rap* p0316–0317 |
| **Vocal trademark** | A signature sound or word placed before or after a particularly forceful line to cue the listener (Pusha-T's "yeeuk"). What suits one MC may not suit another | *How to Rap 2* p0154 |

★ `vocal.ad_libs` gives positions in three categories: **keywords, last word of each bar, and hook stacking**. **Do not scatter them evenly.**

### 5.3 Vocal Sound-Effect Inventory (Ad-lib Material)

| Category | Examples | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Imitating vinyl scratching** | Chicka-chicka-chicka; also imitating the sound of a record rewinding quickly | *How to Rap 2* p0145 |
| **Imitative noise** | Sirens (whoop-whoop), tire squeals, video-game sounds, chainsaws | *How to Rap 2* p0145 |
| **Narrative scene sounds** | Drills, car horns, trains, skidding tires; doors slamming, explosions | *How to Rap 2* p0146 |
| **Beatboxing** | Imitating a drum kit with the mouth and playing it in different rhythms. Usually accompanied by a dedicated beatboxer, occasionally embedded by the MC in a rap section | *How to Rap 2* p0146 |
| **Interjections** | Uh / Huh / Ahh / Mmm / Ha / Um: **provide extra syllables or noise to complement an existing rhythm**. They blend well with real words and are often improvised during recording. Andy Cat: inserting hehs, uhs, ohs, heys in the manner of James Brown adds a bit more swing to the rhythm | *How to Rap 2* p0147 |
| **Scat and random rhythmic sounds** | Meaningless syllables used as pure rhythmic material | *How to Rap 2* p0140–0144 |

★ In `roster`, these count as **color or texture**, not melody. In `subtraction_events`, they are the layer most easily added and removed.

### 5.4 The Spectrum Between Rap and Sung

Rap and sung are not a clean cut (*How to Rap 2* p0155–0167). When writing the spec, say which point on the spectrum you are at:

| Form | Method | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Divided by section** | Some sections sung or half-sung, others rapped; strong structure and easy to remember | Brand Nubian opens with a sung section before the rap; the middle of Slick Rick's *La Di Da Di* is an entire sung section; the first verse of LL Cool J's *Zoom* is entirely half-sung |
| **Half-sung chanted section** | Overlapping short phrases chanted repeatedly, breaking the convention of "three verses, one chorus" | Pharcyde's *Runnin'*, "Can't keep running away" |
| **Switching within a verse** | Only certain words half-sung (Snoop's "baaaby") | |
| **Half-sung as an overall style** | A spectrum: close to actual singing (Bone Thugs) → often half-sung (Slick Rick, Nelly) → voice has a melodic quality but doesn't sing (Snoop, Devin) | |
| **Singing someone else's song** | As a standalone section, or embedded in a verse, often with rewritten words for humor. You can also **speak a sung melody as rap** | Slick Rick sings *Sukiyaki*; the Fugees sing *The Gambler* inside a verse; Method Man speaks the words of a Rolling Stones song |
| **Rhythm of half-sung passages** | Often uses **matching patterns** (the same rhythmic figure in every bar), repeated to reinforce the melody. A party track with the same flow pattern for 16 bars is more catchy | Snoop's *Gin and Juice*: one syllable each in the first two beats of every bar, three or four in the last two |
| **Key** | The melody of a half-sung passage must be in the key of the beat. Within a group, division of labor by range: low voices sing the low melody, high voices the high melody (Jurassic 5) | |
| **Deliberate off-pitch singing** | ODB and Biz Markie use it as humor or a wild persona: "knowing music theory makes it hard to deliberately sing off-key" | *How to Rap 2* p0164–0165 |

★ A half-sung section **must also** have `push_pull` (it is still rap's time-feel) and `melody_register` (it already has pitch).

### 6.1 Further Figures and Recipes

Figures from *Dilla Time* that can be copied directly into `offset_ms`, beyond those in SKILL §3.6:

| Technique | Value / method | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Recipe reconstructed by others | DJ Jazzy Jeff tried every quantization setting and nothing was right; the answer was: **push the snare back a little (sounds rushed), and push the hi-hat forward**. Then copy the pattern several times, change each copy slightly, and chain them together | Chapter 9 |
| Another reconstruction | Phonte's drummer friend summarized it as **"kick is late, snare is early"** | Chapter 11 |
| Distance between two pulses | Danielsen measured D'Angelo's *Left and Right*: a shaker or guitar sets one tempo, and the drum loop implies another slightly earlier pulse, **about 85 ms apart**, and the whole song competes for attention between them | Chapter 14 |
| Vocals can also be shifted | 9th Wonder slid a recorded vocal phrase **to the right as a whole** along the timeline, a little, turning "stiff" into "loose": **delay conveys cool** | Chapter 11 |
| Making flams with effects | Split the hi-hat signal in two and delay one path by **11 ms**; the gap between the two sounds is the flam. A noise gate sets the start and end of drum sounds to shape the pocket | Chapter 10 |

## 7. Glossary

| Term | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Third time-feel / Dilla Time** | The time-feel produced by **placing straight and swung side by side at once**; not the midpoint between them, and not a gradual blend | Author's note, Chapter 8 |
| **Conflicted time** | Another way of saying the same thing: rhythmic friction deliberately cultivated for maximum musicality and maximum surprise | Chapter 8 |
| **Timing Correct** | The MPC quantization function; it can be applied to all sounds, some sounds, or none. "Some" is the key word | Chapter 5 |
| **Shift Timing** | Moves one sound as a whole forward or back by ticks | Chapter 8 |
| **Shuffle** | The proportion by which every even sixteenth note is delayed: 50/50 straight, 66/34 triplet swing, 58/42 and 54/46 in between | Chapter 6 |
| **Rushed snare** | The snare sounds ahead of the backbeat (on the order of 65 ms), while the hi-hat holds the grid | Chapter 8 |
| **The lope** | The term for this groove texture: gait-like, sparing with words, leaving things incomplete | Chapter 15 |
| **Half-time feel** | The snare lands on beat 3, so the perceived tempo halves. `tempo` holds the actual BPM; `groove.feel` holds halftime | Drummer's Bible p0109 |
| **Break** | A stretch of a record where only drums (or drums and percussion) remain; early DJs isolated it and looped it | Schloss p0046–0051 |
| **Looping** | Sampling one or more bars and repeating them with almost no change | Schloss p0121 |
| **Chopping** | Cutting into smaller fragments and reordering them so that continuous performance sounds like collage | Schloss p0166 |
| **Flipping** | Any substantial creative change | Schloss p0179 |
| **Truncate** | Fix the start and end points of a sample | Chapter 5 |
| **Locking up** | Elements enter a self-sustaining relationship: **a beat only counts once it has locked** | Schloss p0154 |
| **Stabs** | Short sounds that pierce the surface texture and quickly fade (guitar stabs, horn stabs); the most common unit for adding and removing | Schloss p0217 |

| **Cyclic sensibility** | Even without loop technology, the form is designed to create an impression of cycling | Schloss p0220 |
| **Live (in this sense)** | **Rhythmically exciting**, not "played on real instruments" | Schloss p0221 |
| **Heterogeneous sound ideal** | The ideal texture is a combination of many timbres (Olly Wilson) | Schloss p0165 |
| **Flow** | Rhythm in the lyrics + rhyme; must fit the basic rhythm of the music | *How to Rap* p0076 |

| **Ad-lib** | Improvised comments mixed into the background | *How to Rap* p0295 |

## 8. 7.2 ★ Elements of Flow: How It Lands on the Grid
This subsection covers the **relation of flow to the beat**. That relation determines how the vocal line in `vocal.phrasing` and `groove.push_pull` is written. How rhyme density and multisyllabic rhyme are built is a matter for songwriting.

**Two foundations:**
- **Stresses on the beat:** unless the beat is a rest, **stressed syllables must sound at the same time as beats 1, 2, 3, and 4**. Only the stresses that fall on the four beats make words and music synchronize; this is called **in the pocket** (*How to Rap* p0084–0085).
- **Basic grid:** the most common rap rhythm divides a bar into **16 parts**. At mid tempo it sounds like everyday speech; at fast tempos it becomes rapid-fire talk (*How to Rap 2* p0023–0027).

**Usable techniques** are in `reference.md` §3.3–§3.5: 32nds, triplets, flams, lazy tails, staccato, crossing bars at the start or end, pushing or pulling the whole line, and rewriting for non-4/4 meters and 3-bar loops. **Rests** serve four purposes: breathing, dividing sections, adding rhythmic variation, and emphasizing content. **Rhyme landing positions** most often fall on beat 4; landing on a weak beat sounds more conversational.

**How to write this into ARR-SPEC:**
- `groove.push_pull` gives the rap vocal a line: `{part: rap_vocal, offset_ms: +20, note: "whole line dragged, Snoop-style; lazy tail on the final word marked separately in phrasing"}`. ★ To judge rushing or dragging, look at the **vowel** position (`mc-vocal-direction`). In rap, the more practical criterion is **the stressed syllable on the beat** (*How to Rap* p0085).
- `vocal.phrasing` is written line by line: which line rushes, which drags, which has a lazy tail, which is staccato. **Use the flow diagram from `reference.md` §3.1 for notation**: one line per four beats, stresses in bold, empty space meaning rest. Placeholder syllables (da / X) are enough. **Setting the flow first and filling in words later is standard practice** (§3.2).
- `vocal.breath_points`: breaths **must be written into rests**. If you run out of breath after a trial run, delete words to make space. When writing, leave breath points, otherwise "the rhyme doesn't sound right" (*How to Rap* p0141, p0285).
- `form[].note`: when the meter or loop length is not a multiple of 4, state it, and note that the rhyme scheme has been adjusted to match.

## 9. 7.3 ★ Dimensions of Delivery: Allocating Them to Sections
*How to Rap 2* breaks delivery into dimensions that can be allocated section by section. **The sound is the first thing listeners hear, and it decides whether they keep listening** (p0072). The eight dimensions are **overall pitch, pitch variation, per-word pitch, volume, timbre, duration, stuttering/repetition, and breath**. Levels, effects, and examples for each dimension are in `reference.md` §4.1. Persona types (wild / bouncy / authoritative / relaxed / introspective / angry, that is, the option set for `vocal.persona`) are in §4.2. Accents and character voices are in §4.3. The allocation logic:

- For sections where the backing is **dense**, use **high pitch** (it cuts through); for serious sections, use **low** pitch (weight and authority). Sections that should make room for the beat use monotone (which sounds more like percussion); emotional sections use varied pitch (p0078–0081).
- A verse's **first line uses rising pitch** (sliding in, a growing sense of urgency), and its **last line uses falling pitch** (strong statement, sense of ending) (p0112–0123).
- Volume: ★ do not write "louder" (`mc-vocal-direction` §2.2). Write **which section uses which level** (p0082–0091). For timbre, when the backing has many instruments, tune the voice to sound "like one of the instruments," **flowing with the beat rather than cutting across it** (p0093–0096).
- Stretch the opening word of a phrase to launch a section; stretch the final word to let the sound linger (**stretching pushes the later words back**). Stutters and repetitions go at section entries and at **fill positions**; plan breath for long verses (p0125–0153).

**Rules:** a hook may change voice often while the verse stays consistent (*How to Rap* p0263); use **punch-in** to switch voice for different roles or situations (p0289); a take recorded in one go keeps its cracks and flaws, which makes it "more real, more raw" (p0293); emotion must be **audible in the voice** (*How to Rap 2* p0172–0175). The parallel point from Dilla's side: "say less, leave things incomplete" (Chapter 15).

## 10. 7.4 Rap Section Structure: Verse, Hook, Bridge, Ad-lib
**Default numbers:** verse **16 bars** ("a 16," the formula is three 16s; story rap caps at 32, *How to Rap* p0206–0209). The hardest parts of a verse are **bars 1–2, 9–10, and 15–16** (p0210). Hook **4 or 8 bars**, with simpler words than the verse, lower density, and slightly stretched. **Melodic hooks are more popular than spoken-rap hooks** (p0200–0202). A hook melody often has its first phrase rise like a "question" and its second fall like an "answer" (*How to Rap 2* p0162–0164). Ad-libs most often **only stack keywords**: Twista doubles only **the last word of each bar** (*How to Rap* p0295).

For the rest of the detail (a hook preset by the producer determines the content direction of the whole song; back-and-forth vocals and posse cuts; where the hype man fills in; vocal trademarks; extreme layering cases; the vocal sound-effects inventory covering scratches, imitative noise, beatbox, and interjections), see `reference.md` §5.1–§5.3.

**How to write this into ARR-SPEC:** `form[].bars` uses 16 / 8 / 4 by default, but lint rule 3 still requires at least one section that is not a multiple of 8. Break it with a 12-bar verse, a 3-bar loop, or a 2-bar breakdown before the hook. `vocal.ad_libs` gives positions in three categories (**keywords, last word of each bar, hook stacking**), **not spread evenly**. `vocal.stacking` states how many layers the hook has and how many the verse has. `vocal.chorus_variation` uses the dimensions of §7.3 to say whether each pass of the hook changes pitch level, volume level, or timbre.

## 11. 7.5 ★ Relationship Between Vocals and Backing
This subsection answers "how the `roster` is assembled for a rap section."

**① The beat leads the writing.** Most MCs write to the final beat (Dray: "like dancing, the beat is the lead dancer"). "That is the template for everything you will say over it" (*How to Rap* p0181–0183). If the beat already contains a **vocal sample**, it directly steers the content (p0183). **Changing the beat changes the stresses**: if you record vocals and then swap the beat, "the stresses and words are emphasized according to the original music" (p0187).

**② The MC is another instrument on the track.** Buckshot: "There are five elements on the beat, and I'm the sixth" (*How to Rap 2* p0012). Thes One: think like a producer, asking "what would it sound like if one more layer of percussion were added," and then build the rhyme scheme around that rhythmic figure (p0021). Evidence: "The way I flow is the way I build drums" (*How to Rap* p0191).

**③ Which element to follow.** MCs usually choose one of the bass line, drums, or piano as a guide (*How to Rap* p0128). They may also switch to following the bass line at a breakdown (Schloss p0188). **When writing the spec, specify which track the rap vocal follows**, since this determines whether the reference frame for the vocal's `push_pull` is the hat or the bass.

**④ Trading density.** "A pure MC sees rhyme as 100% of the song; we see it as 50% rhyme, 50% beat." Adding too many elements gets cluttered, they mask one another, and it turns to mud (*How to Rap 2* p0125, p0145). When the backing already has a lot of low end, **do not add low end to the vocal** (*How to Rap* p0269). Too many samples and the MC gets lost (Schloss p0185). → In bars where the rap is dense, pull back the texture layers; in bars where the rap is sparse or has many rests, bring the texture layers in (§6.7).

**⑤ The vocal must be in key.** If the speaking pitch of the rap clashes badly with the beat's key, it sounds harsh. Adjust until no dissonance is audible (*How to Rap* p0267–0268). Deliberate off-pitch singing is a separate matter, but a melodic hook should be pitched to the MC's overall pitch (*How to Rap 2* p0155, p0164–0165).

**⑥ Matching the time-feel.** The vocal can be shifted back as a whole (Chapter 11). Busta Rhymes **changed his rhyme scheme** to fit the spasmodic kick's "stumbling step" (Chapter 7). In Kendrick's *Complexion*, before the third verse the straight beat fades out, leaving only chords; a few seconds later an "internally conflicted" beat lurches in. **Changing the time-feel underneath the same vocal** can be written into `form[].events` (Chapter 15). A swing that comes from a sample disappears when the beat is swapped out (*How to Rap 2* p0044).

**⑦ The vocal can come first.** An MC can record to a click track and then build the beat around the vocal: "treat the vocal as an instrument and build around it, like a remix" (*How to Rap* p0193–0194). Slum Village's *Fan-Tas-Tic* verse was recorded first against an empty click (Chapter 7).
