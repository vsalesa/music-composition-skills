# Salsa and timba · two-bar clave, interlocking ensemble, living form

**First declare the branch:** son/son montuno, salsa dura, salsa romántica, charanga, songo, contemporary timba, or a documented fusion. "Salsa" is not a single metronomic MIDI groove; timba has coordinated pattern changes ("gears") distinct from generic salsa accompaniment.

## Rhythm-section system

| Role | Salsa/son orientation | Contemporary timba possibilities |
|---|---|---|
| Clave | Declare son/rumba and **2–3 or 3–2**, orientation over two bars | Same care for direction, with rhythmic group changes |
| Bass | Tumbao anticipates harmonic targets and shapes negative space | More varied, sometimes clave-marking tumbaos; may align with a separate kick |
| Piano | Guajeo/montuno: syncopated voicings, rhythmic contour **across 2 bars** | Often longer, contrasting or displaced tumbaos and intentional gear shifts |
| Congas | Slap, muted/open and heel-toe gestures as different articulation classes | Changing marcha and stronger arranged transitions |
| Bongó/bell | Martillo or bell family, often changing role with montuno | Bells and drums coordinate in new gear |
| Timbales/kit | Cascara, bells, fills, and punctuation by **section** | Drum kit can be more independent and drive cambios |
| Horns/voice | Coro-pregón, moñas/solos and arranged answers | Bloques, effects, chant/singer-band interplay with strong sync hits |

## Clave and phrase alignment

- One clave spans **two ordinary 4/4 studio bars** for the standard son representation; count exact cycles according to the host. Different notation systems often express the same music at half/double BPM.
- Choose **direction from the composition**. The two-side and three-side are meaningful musical functions, not the order of a generic array; instruments may imply the clave without playing literal strokes.
- Before writing code draw a two-bar interaction chart showing bass attack points, piano attacks, strong percussion, lead rests and transitions. Do not use a salsa rhythm under an arbitrary 4-bar progression without considering harmonies across clave boundaries.
- If a new melody starts on an upbeat, that is not automatically "contra-clave". Evaluate phrase accents, implied clave and resolution over both bars; avoid simplistic note-by-note "must coincide with clave" validators.
- Chord anticipations and harmonic rhythm can conflict with phrase boundaries; plan a real resolution rather than shifting every chord mechanically on beat 1.

## A professional guajeo, not chord spam

Start with harmonic voicings in a playable piano register; choose inversion per chord to minimise unnecessary jumps. Write a short **rhythmic-melodic figure**, not a full chord repeated in every slot. The guajeo can use two-note dyads, arpeggiated fragments, a high answer and an occasional doubled octave as appropriate. Maintain a two-bar rhythmic identity; vary the figure when the harmony changes. Avoid occupying the singer's entire midrange. In transitions, *drop* piano or simplify it when horns answer.

Bass must agree with the **harmonic destination**, not just the current root at a fixed sixteenth index. If the player anticipates the next chord, write that pitch against the intended upcoming harmony; keep note lengths and attacks distinct from conga/kick. Test piano+bass alone, then add each percussion part.

## Timba needs genuine coordinated section changes

1. Establish a basic marcha or first gear with all rhythm-section roles.
2. Compose an alternate gear differing in **several linked parts**: conga pattern, bell/kit, bass figure, keyboard tumbao and vocal energy—not an unrelated random solo fill.
3. Choose a transition trigger: singer call, ensemble stop, short effect/bloque or held harmonic turn.
4. Introduce the gear together on a defined bar and sustain it long enough to feel different.
5. Return by a planned pickup. "every 4 (fast 2)" added to one percussion track is usually not a meaningful gear change.

Use the presence/absence of independent kick thoughtfully in timba; the genre label alone does not mandate a kick on all four beats. Distinguish **marcha arriba/abajo** and local variations using reference recordings, not a universal formula.

## Song architecture

**Salsa dura:** intro motif → verse/cuerpo → entrance of coro and montuno → instrumental moña / solo → coro-pregón exchanges → mambos or dynamic turn → resolved ending. **Romántica:** foreground sustained lead line, restrained early percussion and gradual brass/refrain lift. **Timba:** extended dynamic chorus/montuno with multiple gears and breaks, more abrupt planned changes. These are flexible maps, not mandatory fixed bar counts.

Arrange horn sections with real breath and textural contrast: choose lead voice, harmony spacing, rhythm-unison punctuation and rest. A melody and countermelody should *converse*, not compete. On each repeated coro, change one significant dimension (instrument ownership, density, counterline, dynamics or harmonic colour), preserving the coro identity.

## Failure tests

- Can the 2-bar clave orientation be explained and the accompaniment heard against it? Do not demand audible clave sample in every bar.
- Are conga, bass and piano **interlocking**, each doing a separate job, and is the bass anticipation harmonically correct?
- Are the piano notes coherent voicings rather than all-chords-all-the-time?
- Is the section truly montuno/timba gear or merely an electronic drum fill?
- Does a real main theme return with transformation?
- With sample instruments muted one at a time, does the groove still retain its specific rhythmic grammar?
