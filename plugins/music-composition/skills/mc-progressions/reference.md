## 1. J-POP Major Type (bright, catchy, major-key loops)

| Roman numerals | Key=C | Mood / context | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| I–IV–V–I | C–F–G–C | Direct, simple, easy to fit lyrics to | **The king of kings.** Takeuchi Mariya's "Genki wo Dashite," Shonan no Kaze's "Junkoi Uta" |
| IV–V–I–VIm | F–G–C–Am | Ace for opening a chorus, grand | Starting from IV tends to form a strong pull toward the tonic; also common in dance music |
| IV–V–VIm–I | F–G–Am–C | Bright with a touch of setsunai | Swept Japanese pop in the 1990s; **paired with the previous one, it fine-tunes the amount of setsunai** |
| I–VIm–IV–V | C–Am–F–G | Cute, playful | Strongly loop-based; easy fingerings |
| I–IV–VIm–V | C–F–Am–G | Accent for a transition | The surprise of Am after C–F; **most effective used only once, at the end of a lyrical bridge** |

| Roman numerals | Key=C | Mood / context | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| I–III7–IV–IVm | C–E7–F–Fm | Brightness and setsunai coexist | Contains two notes borrowed from minor; used from Japanese folk songs to British pop |
| I–IVm–I | C–Fm–C | **Guaranteed to produce a good melody** | **The classic use of the minor subdominant (IVm)**; the bass often stays on I during the IVm |

| IV△7–IIIm7–IIm7–I△7 | F△7–Em7–Dm7–C△7 | Pop / R&B standard | Known from "Lovin' You"; **inserting a #IVm7(♭5) before looping back is very stylish** |

| I–VIIm7(♭5)·III7–VIm–v7·I | C–Bm7(♭5)·E7–Am–Gm7·C | Grand, high information density | The drop creates grandeur; **played fast, it becomes the hard push of "Mixnuts"** |
| I–v7·I7–IV–IIm·V | C–Gm7·C7–F–Dm·G | Warm emo | Gm7–C7 = ii–V pointing to IV; surprise plus heart-fluttering |
| IIIm–VIm–II7–V | Em–Am–D7–G | Trigger into the next section | D7 is a secondary dominant; the driving phrase in the B section of Shiina Ringo's "Koufukuron" |

## 9. Five Practical Lessons (consensus of the three source books)

1. **Substitute chords interchangeably**: same-function chords can be swapped (T: C/Em/Am; SD: F/Dm; D: G/Bm(♭5)).
   G→Bm(♭5) adds plaintiveness, Em→G adds loopiness, C→Am darkens the ending, and C→Em adds complexity.
   **Changing just one chord can change the color of the story**; the melody may clash, so **ultimately let the ear decide**.
2. **Loop vs. resolve**: to loop, put the **D function at the end** of the progression so it flows back to the opening tonic; to resolve, land on T.
   Functional flow is the kishōtenketsu (introduction, development, turn, conclusion) between stability (T) and instability (SD/D).
3. **Secondary dominants are the first weapon for bringing in non-diatonic notes**: A7→Dm, B7→Em, C7→F, D7→G (double dominant), E7→Am. Usually in seventh-chord form.
4. ★ **The bass determines chord function**: with the same upper chords, changing the bass changes the whole progression (upper C triad + bass A = Am7). **Working backward from the bass line is also a valid compositional method.**
5. ★ **The top voice is the melody**: the ear treats the highest note as the melody. The same progression can be **re-designed as a top-voice line through arrangement (inversion)**: **"changing the arrangement itself is composing."**
